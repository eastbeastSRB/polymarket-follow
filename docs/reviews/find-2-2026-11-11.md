## For the owner, in one page

**The answer, in one sentence.** Of the 100 accounts that banked the most in
the 30 days before 7 November (among those whose full history we hold), about
**a fifth** of the profit came from the part a follower could at best share —
but the test **cannot settle** whether that part is really below half, so
entry 2 does **not** go to the exams.

*What the numbers say* (list (c): 87 accounts with a closed position, 2,107
positions, $694,686 of profit):
- **Two thirds of the profit (68%) came from positions held longer than a
  day.** The entry counts that as "not followable", yet a follower who copied
  the entry and held as long could in principle have shared it. Counted with
  it, the shareable part is 89%. But this is mostly **five accounts**: they
  hold 86% of that long-hold profit; without them, long holds are a quarter of
  the rest's profit, and the shareable part is about half.
- **What no follower can copy was small for these accounts:** better prices
  than the market's at the moment of the trade (4%), lower fees or rebates
  (4%), the first two minutes after a trade (5%) and funding (3%).
- **For #47 and R8's 50 — the fast traders R8 tested — it is the opposite.**
  81% of #47's profit came in the first two minutes after its trades, and its
  better prices and lower fees added 10% each; what was left for a follower
  barely paid the ordinary fees (about 1% of its profit). A follower who
  bought and sold like an ordinary taker would have lost money on what was
  left. That is why #47 could make money while its follower lost. (Priced by
  the second where R8's recording has it, more of #47's profit falls just
  after the two-minute mark — the minute's price hides the third minute — so
  the shareable part is larger, but still not enough to pay a taker's costs.)

*Why "not settled".* The profit sits in a few accounts (the largest holds 30%
of it) and a few days. Resampled honestly, the share could plausibly be as
high as 62% (by accounts) or above 100% (by days), so the line — below half
with room to spare, by both readings — is not met, also without the
November competition's days or markets.

*What it means.* Board profit is not followable profit: for the fastest
traders almost none of it can be copied; for the top accounts of these 30
days most of it came from a few long holds. Nothing here says what to trade.

## What is measured (written before the look)

*What is measured.* A top account's profit has parts a follower cannot have:
buying below the fair price or selling above it at the moment of the trade
(**execution**), paying lower fees than an ordinary account or earning rebates
(**the fee gap**), the price moves of the first two minutes after any trade
(nobody can copy that fast), and **funding**. The part a follower could at
best share is the **holding between two minutes and a day** — the price
moves while the position is between two minutes and 24 hours old — minus the
ordinary fees on those trades. That is the **followable profit**. The study
splits every closed position of three lists into these parts, to the dollar:
#47 (the account that made $109,386 while its follower lost), R8's 50, and
the 100 accounts that banked the most in the 30 days before 2026-11-07 (of
the accounts whose stored history reaches back that far: the 91 busiest,
whose history was cut short by the original download's page limit, cannot be
ranked fairly and are left out).

*What would count as a finding.* That the followable part is less than half
of the top 100's profit — with room to spare, so that luck, a few large
accounts or a few lucky days cannot explain it, and not only thanks to the
volume competition of November. If so, a ranking by profit says little about
what a follower can have, and a second check — whether an account's
followable profit per dollar repeats in the next weeks, and not only through
its fees or inside the competition — goes to the exams.

*What it can see.* With profit this concentrated (the top account of the 100
holds 23% of their profit, the top five 54%) the test is strict: it settles
the question only if the followable share is low — near zero or negative — and
otherwise reports "not settled". That is the honest price of not being fooled
(section 1).
