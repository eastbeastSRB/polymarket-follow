## For the owner, in one page

*Words.* A **bp** is 0.01%. A **risk unit** is a market's usual move over a
day: its average 24-hour move, up or down, over the 30 days before the trade.
Results are divided by it, so that a quiet market and a wild one weigh
alike. A **p-value** is how often chance alone would give a result at least
this far from zero. The line it has to beat is 0.05.

*The question.* Some directional traders usually keep a position for six
hours or more. These are the traders a follower could actually copy. Suppose
a follower copied each new position of theirs about 80 seconds late and held
it for a day. Would that have beaten a random trade, meaning the average of
every public trade in the same market and hour, taken long and short in the
trader's own usual mix? This yardstick gives no credit for simply being long
while the market rose.

*The answer: no.* The data run from 2026-09-03 to 2026-11-07: 951 such
trades by 73 traders on 64 days. Against random trades they came out at
**−0.04 and −0.01 risk units** on the two averages that decide (one counts
each day once, the other each trader once). In bps that is **0 and +9 bps** a
trade. Chance alone would give results this far from zero about half the
time or more (p = 0.53 and 0.88). **The study does not pass.**

*How sure.* The test could catch an edge of about **0.21 risk units (about 43
bps a trade over a day) four times in five**. Nothing near that showed. The
data still allow anything from about 0.15 risk units worse than random to
0.08–0.14 better (in bps, about −23 to +23 on the day average and −21 to +40
on the trader average). A small edge, about the size that would just pay a
taker's costs, cannot be ruled out by 65 days. The exams could not have
confirmed one either (below).

*The checks agree.*
- Random trades put in place of the real ones passed the test 4 times in
  1,000, so the test is strict, not loose.
- Moving every trade a day earlier or a day later shows nothing.
- Leaving out any one trader, market or day changes nothing: no version
  passes.

*What copying would have earned (paper only).* This is each trade's own
result after costs and funding, not compared with random trades. After a
taker's costs it is about −13 bps a trade on the day average and −9 on the
trader average, or +10 when every trade weighs alike. As a maker it is −5,
−1 and +17. None of these can be told apart from zero. They measure the
past and are not advice.

*Other views, deciding nothing.*
- Holding 6 hours or 72 hours instead of a day shows nothing either.
- The 33 accounts that came into our pool through profit boards did a
  little better (+0.05 and +0.12 risk units, not significant). That is what
  accounts picked because they had made money in these very weeks would show.

of 2026-11-30 and 2026-12-19, and it adds nothing to the batch size m, so

## 7. What stays unknown

  over a day). The 65 days cannot tell it from zero, and the exams' 29
  accounts could not see it either.
- **Whether slow traders differ among themselves.** An average near zero can
  hide good traders and bad ones. That is FIND-1's question, and its P1
  scores these entries too.
- **Following the trader's own exits instead of a fixed day.** Not tested:
  the entry's horizon is fixed at 24 h, with 6 h and 72 h beside.
- **Other periods.** The 65 days end inside the competition. The beta weeks
  before 2026-09-03 are left out, and the new weeks are not read.
- **The owner's own cost setting.** It is not yet defined; the net prints
  use the taker and maker settings only. Funding's share of the net result
  is not printed apart.
